We grew up in a society living in the twentieth century, where education had been arranged into many subjects. One of them was religion, a part of social studies. Most of it was Buddhism, with other religions added only as a formality. The people who designed the lessons said that Buddhism was Thailand’s national religion, so we should study it in depth. Those of other faiths had to follow along too. And so, for a long time, I saw Buddhism, with Prince Siddhartha as its teacher, as the highest truth. I looked down on other religions as blind belief, empty of any real meaning. This was the view I held for years.

When I began reading books, something that seems hard to do these days, along with material online, I happened upon the idea of ‘God.’ Those who believed in one God tried to explain and persuade others of what they believed. They used logic that seemed to make sense, and science, which most people admire and hold in high regard, as tools to spread their belief. They did this rather well. Their reasons seemed sound and deeply convincing. I asked: If God is real, where is he? Why does he not appear right here, if he truly exists? Their answer was that we are created beings. Our very existence already confirms that God exists. In short, they drew me further and further toward this idea. But that meant rejecting Buddhist teachings altogether. I talked with them and quietly bought their holy book to study in earnest, with a deep sense of wonder.

Buddhists, and followers of Eastern religions in general, often believe in ‘emptiness’ or ‘no self,’ ideas that matter greatly in the thought of this part of the world. Whether it is ‘Nibbana,’ ‘Nirvana,’ ‘Tao,’ or ‘non-self,’ none involves the idea of ‘God, who is the beginning and the end.’ They believe that whether God exists or not has nothing to do with their freedom from suffering. These things shook my belief. In brief, half of me was with God, and the other half with Nirvana. You could put it that way.

This, then, is my curse. I am not firm enough to choose one belief. On the side of God, I believe he may truly exist. But the other side says that this is not freedom from suffering. Worse, it may be ignorance itself, something foolish. Everything falls under the three marks of existence: change, suffering, and no self. Yet the side that believes in God says that what I understand now is the work of Satan, the tempter, leading me astray. ‘Do not speak with him. Believing in God alone is the true way to be saved.’

Please, all of you, when I am dying, or when I have already died, I beg you: let one group chant, meditate, and offer the merit of their good deeds to me. Let the other group ask the Lord to forgive me. I do not wish to burn in hell for ages without end. I ask for your mercy. Please take pity on this creature, whether you see it as a lowly beast or a noble being.
