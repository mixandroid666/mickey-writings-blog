When I was little, I did not know that everyone had to die. We would live to play forever, eat delicious food, and have our mothers sing us to sleep every night.

Perhaps it was something in the news, or seeing old people and dead people. I cannot say why, but I asked my mother, ‘Do people really have to die?’ The answer was yes. That knowledge made a child of five or six pause for a moment. Then, as children do, I forgot.

As I grew up, the truth of that day became more and more real. People close to me, people I loved, and friends left us one by one. With social media, the news came faster and faster. It made me stop and think about death again.

At first, I thought death was frightening. It must be full of darkness and emptiness. We would never meet anyone again, not even ourselves. It was loneliness, utterly alone. But when I really thought about it, I could turn this meaning into ‘peace,’ ‘a quiet heart,’ or ‘rest.’ And so my fear of death slowly began to change.

But there is something more frightening: the time before death. This is real. You can still feel it fully, both in your body and in your heart. It affects those around you too, not only the person who is dying. So I try to learn how to face this event, which none of us can avoid.

I imagine that people who are about to die, whether in an accident on the road, in a hospital bed, or even at the hands of a murderer, must be full of fear and sorrow. If it were us, what would we do?

If I could choose, I would rather pass through this stage quickly. But since I cannot choose, I must accept it and find a way to face it. Many religions and beliefs offer to help with this. Most Eastern religions place value on the ‘last state of mind’: the dying person’s final feelings will shape their next life. So we must be joyful before death and let go of dark feelings. But who can do that when they are already dying? That is why we need to practice for this journey.

When we prepare to die, or know that we are going to die, religion and belief can do a great deal as something for the heart to hold on to. Of course, this cannot be proved through science. But we can see its effect on the mind. Many people who have come through an encounter with death have brought some kind of belief into their lives, though not everyone has.

What if we say, ‘I will believe, or choose a religion, when I am close to death. For now, let me keep living this way’? I once thought like that. But when I had an experience close to death, or thought that I was dying, I fell apart, filled with fear. We can no longer choose what to believe or not believe. The mind goes wrong. It is confused. It cannot work. So what I want to suggest is this: belief, religion, miracles, the law of karma, the cycle of rebirth, God—all of these take time before they flower and bear fruit.

I chose to become ‘a person of faith’ because I am too weak and too afraid to leave this world alone with nothing to hold on to. At first, I thought I was so strong that nothing could shake my heart. This makes me sound like a hypocrite. I say I believe, but I believe for this reason or that. When I am truly dying, perhaps it will not help much because I do not really believe.

Of course, I ask myself this too. To accept the ideas of a religion or a belief, must we truly believe before they can take effect in our hearts? This is a delicate matter, especially in an age when society places science above all. We tend to let reason lead. Things must be proved by scientific methods. I am one of those people. And since religion often includes miracles, it tends to clash with these values.

But we often forget that we do not believe these things with the brain. We believe them with the heart. We do not need to prove everything. We do not need to run an experiment and get the same result every time. This is not mathematics. It is something much deeper. So I study and open my heart to this space.

It turns out that things are not so bad when we believe with the heart rather than the brain. I have found many wonders beyond what the mind can grasp. Some are things that happen to us every day, but the way we see them changes completely. Simply open your heart and use it to search, and you may find these things without much difficulty.

Over time, my spiritual journey has slowly grown, little by little. When I look back, I find that I have come quite a long way. But is it far enough to face the vast emptiness of death?

Returning to death once more, I cannot know whether this is the right way to prepare for it. But it suits me. At least when that time comes, I will probably still fall apart. Yet in the middle of that wild sea and storm, perhaps I will see a great tree in the distance, something to cling to, however little. Or perhaps, when I have grown further in spirit, ‘death’ or ‘the time before death’ will be only an ordinary passage, one I can ‘admire,’ ‘welcome,’ and ‘celebrate.’ For I already believe that it does not end here. It is only one part of the process. There is more!
