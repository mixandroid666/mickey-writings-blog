*2013 AD*

“You’re making me listen to this shit again? I can’t make sense of it. No taste at all. Just screaming and yelling.”

Breeze complained to a friend as they sat on one of the school’s stone benches in the evening, something we high school students did regularly. This guy was always finding strange songs—or at least songs Breeze thought were “strange”—and offering them to him whenever the chance came up. Which meant every lunchtime and evening.

“This band isn’t like the others. I think you’ll like them. The music isn’t gloomy. You’ll definitely get it.”

He kept selling it to Breeze with a sly grin. Who knew why he tried so hard to force this stuff on him? And only on Breeze, too.

His name was “Phu.” Tall and lanky, dark-skinned, and always sporting a buzz cut. Most importantly, he was a “guitarist!” So this was the curse of the high school guitarist. Breeze came up with a hundred and one reasons to explain to himself why guitarists had to play rock, or mainly listen to rock. Why not jazz, classical, or even country? What was so special about this music he could not understand? Why were so many people so taken with it?

“All right, all right. What song is it?”

“Afff-terrr-life, by Avenged Sevenfold!”

Phu made his voice deep and grand, like an announcer introducing a boxer to the ring. He handed over a pair of earphones, some brand sold at a well-known convenience store, already plugged into a red-and-white Nokia 5300 XpressMusic slider phone. All that was left was to press play.

“Okay. Give me a minute.”

Breeze pushed the earphones into his ears. They were in-ear ones, with rubber tips good enough to block the noise around him and let him hear more of the music’s details.

Before long, he took them out and started asking his friend questions.

“Rock music uses orchestral strings too?”

“Whoa, hey! The chorus has all these vocal parts going at once.”

“Those chords are fucking beautiful.”

“The singer’s fucking good. It’s not just mindless screaming. You can actually follow it. High notes with that thick sound—that’s hard to find.”

Questions kept bursting out of Breeze’s mouth. This time, listening to a rock band was different. Maybe the earphones were better than the thin sound from a phone speaker. Or maybe it was because Phu’s earlier choices had been Slipknot, Lamb of God, or even Cannibal Corpse, music that could be harder to get into.

Phu stood with his arms folded, his sly grin wider than ever, like a man who had won without having to do very much.

“What was the band’s name again?”

“Avenged Sevenfold.”

“That’s a fucking long name.”

“Send it to me over Bluetooth. Send me their other songs too.”

“You got it, mate. They’ve just put out a new album. I’ll send that too. Nightmare.”

From then on, Breeze threw himself into studying the art called “music,” with a more open mind. He focused on rock and metal, then narrowed it further to Avenged Sevenfold in particular.

Breeze especially liked studying chords and vocal arrangements, because he liked playing keyboard instruments. This had been a problem, a great wall between him and other kinds of music. He had limited his taste according to the instrument he played. A piano was, of course, a keyboard instrument. So he mainly listened to classical music, jazz, or popular songs in which the piano played the leading role. Classical music and jazz often made heavy use of music theory, which made us look knowledgeable or “classy.” In other words, Breeze thought he was better than other people in this area. He had held on to this value all along, spending his days insulting or judging others.

Avenged Sevenfold were different. They drew him in through things he already loved: beautiful chords and vocal arrangements, perhaps the result of deep thought, planning, and hard work. Breeze also liked singing and singers’ voices, but he had not known a human voice could be fierce and beautiful in this way.

For two or three months, he studied their work like a madman. He dug into the band’s history and downloaded every album from 4shared, from Sounding the Seventh Trumpet to Nightmare, their latest album at the time. He also learned the sad story of their drummer’s death.

Their evening conversations on the school’s stone benches often turned to one A7X song or another. Phu became his companion in all things musical. He would bring an acoustic guitar and play cool A7X riffs. We regularly sang “Seize the Day” and “Dear God,” with the other boys joining in and shouting like mad, our school uniforms soaked in sweat after football.

Breeze began translating the lyrics, trying to understand what the band wanted to say. This made him love them even more. He kept sinking deeper. The wall of his ego had fallen. “Bat Country” and “Beast and the Harlot” had smashed it down. “Second Heartbeat” and “Buried Alive” swept up the rubble and buried it.

More than that, he had taken on the image of M. Shadows. Black became his favorite color. He wanted black Ray-Ban aviators, a bandana, and a cap worn backward. Most of all, he wanted tattoos.

For the first time, he saw beauty in heaviness, sweetness in loud and rough voices, and confidence and being yourself in the music called rock or metal. This spread to other bands. Slipknot’s “Psychosocial” became his morning alarm. “Through Struggle” by As I Lay Dying became his ringtone. He discovered Dream Theater and realized that a keyboard could do more than play Mozart or Tor Saksit.

Phu had done it. A flawless mission. Breeze had become a full metalhead. He screamed and growled like a madman in the classroom for no reason. This was only one symptom of the metalhead virus, a lifelong condition with no cure.

*April 2026 AD (13 years later)*

“Excuse me for a moment.”

A man in his early thirties made an excuse to step out of a meeting at an office in Sathorn, just so he could buy an Avenged Sevenfold concert ticket in time.

“No VIP? Fine. Those went fucking fast. Standing A is good enough. All right, four thousand. I’ll pay it.”

Breeze wanted a seated ticket. Recently, he had started getting severe back pain when he stood for too long. But for the band he had loved and seen as heroes from the start, the pain would be worth it. At least he could nod his head to songs that held precious memories of his youth. Nothing could be more worth it.

He did not invite any of his high school friends. They had been out of touch for so long that reaching out now would feel strange. Some had families and were busy with work. So he decided to go alone, just himself.

“The concert’s still ages away, but the tickets really do sell out fast.”

He half complained as he walked back into the meeting room.

*6 October 2026 (Concert day)*  
*Thunder Dome, Muang Thong Thani*

*3:03 PM*

“There are so many fucking people.”

Those were Breeze’s first words after stepping out of a Geely, some electric car he had booked through an app.

“Seriously? This many?”

He was quite shocked. He had never seen a major international band in concert before. The crowd of Deathbats was huge, as though some great gathering was taking place here.

“That queue is fucking long. Where am I supposed to go?”

He wandered awkwardly, looking clueless, before discovering a special lane for VIP and Standing A ticket holders. His ticket was Standing A.

After getting his physical ticket and wristband, he looked around again.

“What are all those people queuing for? Black everywhere. Looks like a funeral. Ha!”

Black was clearly the main theme of the event.

“Oh, they’re buying shirts. What the hell?”

“That queue’s way too long. How many hours would it take? My back would fucking kill me.”

At 3:30, he decided to go back to his room without buying any merchandise, though he wanted some. He was staying nearby. Coming back when the doors opened seemed like a better idea.

*6:33 PM*

Breeze returned, more excited with every second that brought the show closer. The air was lively, and the sea of black seemed darker still as night slowly crept in. He saw people of many nationalities. Yet everyone seemed to be friends. Here, we all shared something we believed in.

They stood for a while waiting for the doors to open, long enough for his back pain to appear.

“Should’ve got a seated ticket.”

He complained a little, in case it made things better. It did not help much.

“Standing A, this way, please! Come on through!”

A staff member shouted for the people in black to start moving into the hall.

Breeze slowly went in. He had not expected the checks for banned items to be this strict. A little more and it would have been like an airport. Still, he had no real trouble. He only had to remove and throw away his water bottle’s cap.

The stage was enormous. It was the first time he had seen anything this big. He had seen large stages at beer halls and big bars during parties with government staff from his workplace, but their sense of power could not compare with this one. Perhaps it was the excitement of meeting his childhood heroes, people who had helped shape who he was today.

“Oh, we can sit down.”

People around him began sitting in groups on the floor. Because he had a standing ticket, he had thought sitting down might get him scolded or mocked for not being a “real fan.” Relieved, he found somewhere to sit and ease the pain that had been building in his back.

He was not the only one complaining about sore backs or legs. Several fellow fans nearby were doing the same. They were probably around his age, five or six years older or younger at most. Health problems at this age were likely much the same.

Before long, Little John, the opening band for the evening, began to play. Breeze had heard some of their songs, though not all. What struck him was how well they played, both in sound and professionalism. Their singer stood out, with a high voice that climbed and dropped smoothly, seemingly without much effort. It took Breeze back to when music had meant everything to him.

“Why isn’t it him up there?”

Should it not have been him? He thought he could do just as well, perhaps even better than this opening band. For that moment, he took his own side. It was his disappointment in himself, demanding attention:

“Hey, you. I’m your old self. You had something you wanted to be, didn’t you? Like him. You wanted to be like him. But you failed, didn’t you? Go on. Comfort yourself by envying or insulting someone else. You’re good already. Don’t blame yourself. You did nothing wrong. You’ll feel better!”

Only a fraction of a second passed before Breeze felt disgusted with himself. He hated himself to the bone. He had let his ego take control once again. In the end, he accepted that he had failed. He had not reached the shore of the dream he had held when he was young. He was an office worker, only a listener. Since graduation, he no longer performed on a stage. His stage was a meeting table or a desk, and meetings were the concerts he played with his colleagues.

“Get away from meeeee! (F# to A)”

The singer’s piercing high voice carried no strain. It was natural and simple, yet strangely good. It touched something in Breeze once more. It made him see where he should stand, and wonder how he should truly see himself. The sound rang in his ears, slowly chipping away at the shell of his identity. His earlier envy began to return, but at once it turned into praise, acceptance, and letting go. He applauded the band with an open heart. The stage before him was no longer his. He had his own stage behind the scenes, where he believed he did better than anyone else. Though perhaps this acceptance was only the false comfort people often recommend to help someone feel better.

Loud applause mixed with whistles after Little John’s wonderful final song.

The stage lights went out again. Everyone knew this was the final stretch before what the men and women in black had been waiting for, some since the afternoon. A few went out to use the toilets, smoke, or buy more beer. Crew members came onstage to arrange the equipment. Flashing lights revealed the A7X drummer’s kit, set high above the stage, drawing excited cries from the crowd in black.

“♩. ♩. ♩. ♩ ♩.  ♩

D   A   Ab  G  F#  D”

A clear, glasslike sound came out of the darkness. The melody was mysterious and frightening, but familiar. Yes, it was the intro to “Nightmare,” almost a signature of the band. Below the stage, people shouted wildly in the dark. When the drums came in, the stage lights flashed, showing the band members’ shadows as they walked out from the side. The crowd went even wilder.

It was real. Breeze’s dream had come true. His heroes, his role models, were right before him, pounding away as though to remind all of us who we were. What a precious experience. He did not know the setlist. He wanted to meet this fresh experience for himself. He tried to connect his spirit in the present to the spirit of his youth, with this band as a sacred bridge.

“Alive.”

He found himself alive. Of course he was alive already. But this was not the life we live every day. In this moment, it was something more. It had more “reality.” It was so clear, so very real. The high school boy had returned, full of youthful energy and wild joy.

The first song ended, leaving him deeply satisfied. Breeze felt as though his spirit had left his body. He was confused, unable to tell reality from the world in his mind. He stayed dazed for two or three minutes after the song ended. Perhaps it was the very loud sound, with its sharp edge, like someone pushing knives through both ears into his brain, together with the shifting lights he was not used to.

But before long, the next intro began without delay. The strings rose again. It was the same sound he had heard more than ten years earlier. This was the sound that had broken down his wall and rebuilt him. It was like a sacred song that had led him into the world of people in black.

“I need another chance to live”

Those words hit his heart so hard. It was the bridge sung by The Rev, the late drummer who also sang the band’s main backing vocals. The voice was high and rough. Breeze thought of it as the roar of a proud wild beast. He often used this part to fire himself up whenever he was about to do something big.

The band served up their “classic” hits alongside songs from their “new era,” moving happily between slow and fast. Breeze jumped, danced, and shouted along with joy. By the time he noticed, the pain in his back was much worse than usual. The natural painkiller of the first songs’ excitement had slowly worn off as he became aware of himself again.

“A lonely road, crossed another cold state line”

The band surprised the fans with “Dear God,” drawing a great cheer. It was the same song Breeze and his friends had shouted together in some corner of the school in the evening. A flashback appeared easily in his mind. What a wonderful song. It worked as a “time machine,” carrying us back to a past we could never physically return to.

“Decline and fading.”

That would not be a bad description of this moment. Time did its work with great dedication. We humans are born, grow up, get back pain, lose our voices, and die. Some songs clearly remind us of what time does. So did M. Shadows’ voice. It was no longer perfect, at least to Breeze. At times, Matt could not reach the high notes. Sometimes he sang lower instead. But Breeze did not mind. He understood—not just with “sympathy,” but with a deeper kind of “understanding.” We all see our own decline. He saw Matt and Avenged Sevenfold as musical guides and teachers who had helped shape his identity. More than that, he saw them as “friends.” It was an understanding between friends, encouraging one another in the present and thanking one another for the friendship of the past. He wished his high school friends were here, especially Phu, that important friend.

When “Bat Country” began, Breeze could not hold back any longer. He needed to let go. The organizers had firmly reminded everyone that mosh pits were forbidden. They probably did not want anyone hurt or put in danger by them.

A young man with glasses and a fresh face beside him gently nudged him to the right and pointed that way. Earlier, Breeze had exchanged a few words with people around him, including this young man.

“They’re fucking going for it, bro!”

The young man shouted.

Breeze looked over. Without hesitation, he and the young man slipped into the mosh pit that was beginning to form.

“Fuck it!”

They joined this circle of wild fun. There were people from many backgrounds. Besides Thai people, there were Europeans, a Sikh man in a turban, a group of Indonesians, and two or three women, as far as he could gather himself and see through the chaos.

A “mosh pit” is a circle of people who jump around and crash their bodies into one another wildly. It usually happens at rock or metal concerts. Sounds violent, does it not? Of course it is. There is no arguing with that. It is an emotional space for release. These people let their feelings out through “violence” inside this circle. In this ritual, race, gender, and age do not matter. Everyone in the circle is a spirit wanting to reveal something of themselves. How lucky that these spirits have “bodies,” through which they can know “feeling,” “pain,” and “being alive” by way of their senses.

Another strange thing is that whenever someone falls after a collision or slips on the beer spilled across the floor, the spirits around them reach out to help. They keep others from stepping on the fallen person—if that happened, it would be by accident—so the person can get up and crash into everyone again.

Breeze was breathless and having a wonderful time in the mosh pit. “Unholy Confessions” served beautifully as the music for this ritual.

After a while, the show reached its last song. Breeze and the friends around him were breathless and sore from the ritual. His back pain returned to remind him again. His voice was so hoarse that his speech was hardly clear. His smartwatch showed his heart rate at 80 percent of his maximum. He felt as though he had just run as hard as he could.

“Dancin' in the wind

As roses born again

There, you'll find me

Before the dawn of man

In castles made of sand

There, you'll find me”

“Cosmic” reminded us that nothing in the universe lasts. The song spoke of parting and remembering. We found that the pieces of us left behind could live on as memories, friendship, and the spirit of music. Even as they faded, even as time made them disappear, we knew we had been here. Right here. Our timelines had overlapped. We had shared this sacred, precious feeling. And one day, we would have to part. We could not defeat this reality. Yet it was precisely because there was an end that it had value. Dying out and being lost made coming into being and continuing to exist so beautiful. This was true of Avenged Sevenfold themselves, of friends from the past, friends in the present, and the spirits in the mosh pit.

After the concert, Breeze wandered away from the crowd with a clear ringing in his ears. He walked to turn over everything that had happened in the hall. What a wonderful feeling. We had shared the same belief. We had gone back to when we first knew them. We had enjoyed the present with all its wild fun. And we would carry this with us as another memory, as we traveled into the future and eventually faded away.

*7 October 2026*

The next morning, Breeze got up aching all over, with a hoarse voice and patches of bruising on his arms. It was another working day. The night before felt like a dream that had just passed. He slowly made his way to the office, listening to “Dear God” with happiness and peace. Last night, he had taken himself back to a precious past and realized that the music of his youth had never gone anywhere. It had kept beating in his heart all along. He wanted to put this memory into words, in case he forgot it one day when he grew old. Then he could read it, like listening to a song he had heard in his youth.

“While I recall all the words you spoke to me

Can't help but wish that I was there

Back where I'd love to be”

I love you all, Deathbats!
